# Career records, STAR interviews, and AI-assisted resumes/screening: evidence for an AI career-record system (Indian SWE, 2-3 yrs, services to product/GCC)

Confidence labels: HIGH = peer-reviewed or employer-published primary source checked this session; MEDIUM = preprint/working paper or primary source seen only via abstract or index page; LOW = practitioner essay or secondary summary. Per the source policy, no sources that sell to job seekers (resume builders, coaching, interview-prep subscriptions) are relied on. Where only such sources made a claim, this is flagged.

## Q1. Career records: does keeping contemporaneous accomplishment records help, what do they capture, and how does self-promotion/impact framing work?

### Takeaway
No study directly tests "brag documents" or work logs against career outcomes. The case for them is indirect but consistent. Autobiographical memory for everyday events decays substantially over months to years, and recall is best cued by recorded what/who/where/when details. People's unaided self-assessments are systematically inaccurate. Women under-rate their own performance even when there is nothing to gain from it. Julia Evans' practitioner "brag document" sets out a de facto field schema (goals, projects with contribution plus impact plus numbers, collaboration/mentorship, design/docs, company-building, learning, outside work), and it is the main practitioner reference.

### Cited Findings
**Memory and recall decay (MEDIUM: primary papers seen via abstracts and secondary descriptions)**
- Wagenaar, W. A. (1986). "My memory: A study of autobiographical memory over six years." *Cognitive Psychology*, 18, 225–252. He logged about 2,400 daily events over six years, each by *what, who, where, when*, then tested recall by varying which of those served as the cue. Remembering was significantly related to salience, pleasantness, emotional involvement and rehearsal. The retention curve is described as curvilinear (rapid early loss, then slower). — [Springer PDF listing](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03211313.pdf); described in [Journal of Cognition replication article (PMC10798175)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10798175)
- A recent replication of Wagenaar's diary design cued recall of 2,691 diary notes spanning 24 years and found that **fewer than two-thirds of recorded events were remembered**. — [Journal of Cognition, 10.5334/joc.334](https://journalofcognition.org/en/articles/10.5334/joc.334) (search-snippet level; verify exact figures before quoting)
- One secondary summary says cued recall stayed above chance after five years and that who/what/where cues were roughly equally effective. This comes from a flashcard site, so it is LOW and unverified. — [Brainscape flashcards](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/unit-6-autobiographical-memory-history-a-18576082/packs/22684604)

**Self-assessment accuracy (HIGH for existence and scope; content via abstract and press releases)**
- Dunning, D., Heath, C., & Suls, J. M. (2004). "Flawed Self-Assessment: Implications for Health, Education, and the Workplace." *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 5(3), 69–106. DOI 10.1111/j.1529-1006.2004.00018.x. People's self-assessments of skill correlate only modestly with objective performance. Flawed self-assessment occurs "all the way up the corporate ladder". The authors advise policymakers to be wary of self-assessments of skill, expertise and knowledge, and suggest remedies such as requiring people to justify judgments before peers. — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/j.1529-1006.2004.00018.x/); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2005/10/051028135144.htm)
- The same authors' 2018 retrospective notes that designing interventions to improve self-judgment is "worthwhile but complex and nuanced." — [PubMed 29592648](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29592648)

**Gender gap in self-promotion (HIGH)**
- Exley, C. L., & Kessler, J. B. (2022). "The Gender Gap in Self-Promotion." *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 137(3), 1345–1381. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjac003 (NBER WP 26345, 2019). The study ran experiments with more than 4,000 online adults and more than 10,000 school-aged youth. Women describe their performance on a male-typed math/science task less favourably than equally performing men. **The gap persists when all incentives to self-promote are removed**, and it appears by sixth grade. No gap appears on female-typed tasks. — [OUP PDF](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-pdf/137/3/1345/44839891/qjac003.pdf); [NBER w26345](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26345); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:137:y:2022:i:3:p:1345-1381.)

**Brag documents and work logs (LOW: practitioner, non-commercial)**
- Evans, J. (2019, June 28). "Get your work recognized: write a brag document." jvns.ca. The rationale: "you don't remember everything you did", and "your manager (no matter how great they are!) probably doesn't either". Brag documents help with manager changes, promotions and peer review, and they make "fuzzy work" (code-review culture, tech debt) visible. — [jvns.ca](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/)
- Evans' template sections: Goals (this year/next year); Projects (your contributions plus impact: who it helped, numbers, with results added months after launch); Collaboration & mentorship; Design & documentation; Company building (recruiting, interviewing, onboarding); What you learned; Outside of work (talks, OSS, posts). She also gives prompts such as "Did anybody tell you that you did something well recently?" — [jvns.ca](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/)
- On truthfulness: "you don't have to try to make your work sound better than it is… Just make it sound **exactly as good as it is**!" She advises including numbers where possible (time saved, % improvement, number of users) and also counting non-numeric outcomes such as meeting an audit requirement or retaining a key user. — [jvns.ca](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/)
- On cadence: some people update every two weeks, while Evans does one longer session every six months or year, typically one document per year. The doc also serves for reflection: which projects she is most proud of, recurring themes, which work had its intended effect. — [jvns.ca](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/)

### Inferences
- Combining Wagenaar's what/who/where/when cues, Evans' fields and STAR suggests a record schema of date/period, project/context (Situation), own responsibility (Task), own actions with tech/skills (Action), outcome with metric and baseline (Result), collaborators/stakeholders (who), evidence links (PRs, design docs, dashboards, emails), and a follow-up result added later. This is my synthesis, not a validated schema.
- Memory decay favours capturing entries close to the event. Weekly or per-milestone capture is more defensible than an annual reconstruction, though Evans herself reports doing it every 6–12 months.
- Exley & Kessler suggests an AI assistant should give neutral, evidence-anchored prompts ("what changed, by how much, how do you know?") rather than relying on the user's own adjectives. This could partly offset under-claiming, especially for women in a male-typed field like software. The gap does not disappear when incentives are removed, which implies it is partly genuine self-perception, so structured prompting may help more than "be confident" advice.
- Transferability to India: the memory and self-assessment findings are general cognitive findings and plausibly transfer. Exley & Kessler used US samples, and I found no Indian replication. Brag documents arose in US tech culture. Their usefulness in Indian IT services, where client-facing work is often under NDA and individual attribution is weaker, is untested.

### Gaps
- I found **no empirical study** linking accomplishment logs, brag documents or work journals to promotion, ratings, pay or job-search success. All outcome claims are practitioner opinion.
- I did not locate engineering career-ladder documents that require evidence (e.g. public ladders) within the call budget. They need a separate lookup.
- Teresa Amabile & Steven Kramer's diary research ("progress principle") and research on the "recency effect" in manager ratings were not checked this session.
- Exact recall percentages over time for work-specific (rather than general daily-life) events were not found.

## Q2. STAR and behavioural/structured interviews: origins, evidence, what scores well, and how large employers hiring in India run them

### Takeaway
Structured interviews, including behaviour-description (past-behaviour) questions, are among the most valid and fairest interview formats. Their scoring depends on standardised questions, follow-up probes and anchored rubrics. Amazon (employer-published, including on its India site) explicitly tells candidates to answer in STAR, cite data and say "I", not "we". Google publishes its structured interviewing method: behavioural plus hypothetical questions, pre-set follow-ups, and a four-level rubric (poor/borderline/solid/outstanding). The academic origin of the "STAR" acronym itself was not verified this session.

### Cited Findings
**Structured interview research (HIGH/MEDIUM: abstracts and index pages)**
- Levashina, J., Hartwell, C. J., Morgeson, F. P., & Campion, M. A. (2014). "The Structured Employment Interview: Narrative and Quantitative Review of the Research Literature." *Personnel Psychology*, 67(1), 241–293. DOI 10.1111/peps.12052. This narrative plus meta-analytic review covers the 20 years after Campion et al. (1997). It uses Campion, Palmer & Campion's (1997) definition of structure (enhancements that standardise the interview or help interviewers decide what to ask and how to evaluate answers) and their 15-component taxonomy. **Structure reduces the impact of biasing factors (race, gender, disability) on ratings.** Conscientiousness is the trait structured interviews assess most often. One study found applicants were less inclined to accept offers when interviews were highly structured. — [USU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/manage_facpub/423/); [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/peps.12052)
- Huffcutt, A. I., Conway, J. M., Roth, P. L., & Klehe, U.-C. (2004). "The impact of job complexity and study design on situational and behavior description interview validity." *International Journal of Selection and Assessment*, 12(3), 262–273. The meta-analysis covers 54 studies (N ≈ 5,500). **Job complexity lowered the validity of situational (hypothetical) interviews for high-complexity jobs but had no comparable effect on behaviour-description (past-behaviour) interviews.** Predictive designs show about 0.10 lower validity than concurrent designs for both types. — [Giessen repository](https://jlupub.ub.uni-giessen.de/items/043303fd-a40c-4e1f-abc4-3f0f01f78584); [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/31426965)
- Huffcutt et al. (2001) define situational interviews as hypothetical scenarios and behaviour-description interviews as questions about past relevant incidents. — [PDX course copy](https://web.pdx.edu/~mccunee/quant_621/Outlines/Huffcutt%20et%20al%202001(2).doc)
- The US Office of Personnel Management publishes a government (non-commercial) structured interview guide. It was found in search but not fetched. — [OPM structured interviews PDF](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews.pdf)

**Employer-published practice (HIGH)**
- **Amazon (About Amazon India):** "3 tips to ace your Amazon interview" by Brittany Bunch, with tips from Cody Nelson (senior manager, Talent Acquisition); the page is undated. Amazon interviews are behavioural and guided by the Leadership Principles. The page says "Always answer the interview questions using the STAR method", and STAR is "not something to memorize but rather a tool to communicate your strengths and experiences." It advises giving examples of successes *and failures* with lessons learned, adding metrics because Amazon is data-driven, and preferring recent situations. Its sample answer quantifies scale (500 users, 36 countries), satisfaction (9.5/10) and business result. On "I" vs "we": "Let us know what you actually did." — [aboutamazon.in](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/workplace/amazon-interview-success-tips)
- Amazon's careers content defines STAR as "a structured manner of responding to a behavioural interview question by discussing the specific situation, task, action and result". This was seen via search snippets and an amazon.jobs landing page. The amazon.jobs interview-prep URL I tried returned 404. — [amazon.jobs in-person interview landing page](https://origin.gamma.amazon.jobs/en-gb/landing_pages/in-person-interview); [aboutamazon.com LP interview article](https://www.aboutamazon.com/md/news/workplace/amazon-leadership-principles-interview)
- **Google (re:Work, "Guide: Use structured interviewing"):** Google splits questions into *behavioural* ("describe prior achievements", "good at revealing patterns of behavior") and *hypothetical* ones. Example: "Tell me about a time your behavior had a positive impact on your team", with pre-set follow-ups on the goal, teammates' responses and next steps. Rubrics give interviewers "a shared understanding of what outstanding, solid, borderline, and poor response looks like." Interviewers take detailed notes. The attributes assessed include role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership. Google cites research showing structured interviews give "increased predictive validity and decreased differences between demographic groups". Internally, structured interviews were more predictive than unstructured ones, saved about 40 minutes per interview, and rejected candidates who had a structured interview were 35% happier. No coefficients or sample sizes are given. — [rework.withgoogle.com](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/a-guide-to-structured-interviewing-for-better-hiring-practices)

### Inferences
- What scores well, by convergence of Amazon, Google and the structured-interview logic: a **specific, real, recent** episode; the candidate's **own actions** ("I"); **quantified results**; reflection or lessons (including from failures); and enough depth to survive **pre-planned follow-up probes**. A STAR record should therefore store depth beyond the 2-line bullet: alternatives considered, obstacles, numbers with their source, and what you'd do differently.
- Huffcutt et al. (2004) imply that past-behaviour questions keep their validity for complex jobs, which supports investing in a bank of true stories over rehearsed hypotheticals for SWE roles.
- For Indian services-to-product switchers: these employers' processes are global, and Amazon publishes this guidance on its India site, so the guidance plausibly applies to India hiring and GCCs. Services engineers often describe team or client deliverables with "we". The "I, not we" and metric guidance means the record should explicitly separate individual contribution from team outcome.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session (no fetch or search hit): Janz, T. (1982), "Initial comparisons of patterned behavior description interviews versus unstructured interviews," *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 67(5), 577–580; Motowidlo, S. J., et al. (1992), "Studies of the structured behavioral interview," *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 77(5), 571–587; Campion, M. A., Palmer, D. K., & Campion, J. E. (1997), "A review of structure in the selection interview," *Personnel Psychology*, 50(3), 655–702. These are well-known citations, but the details above come from memory and should be verified before quoting. Campion et al. (1997) is confirmed indirectly as the basis of Levashina et al.'s definition.
- **Origin of the STAR acronym**, often attributed to Development Dimensions International (DDI) and "targeted selection", and the variants CAR, SOAR and Google's "XYZ" resume formula ("Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]", attributed to Laszlo Bock): no primary source was verified this session. The search results on these were dominated by commercial interview-prep sites, so treat these attributions as **unverified**.
- **Microsoft's** employer-published interview guidance was not retrieved.
- I found no peer-reviewed study that tests STAR-formatted answers specifically against unformatted answers on interviewer ratings. The evidence is on structured *interviews* (the interviewer side), not on candidate answer formats.
- I found no research on behavioural interviews conducted in India specifically.

## Q3. AI-assisted resume writing and AI-based screening: benefits, homogenisation, signalling, bias, and fabrication risk

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence shows that **non-generative** writing assistance (grammar and spelling fixes) raised hires by about 8% with no drop in employer satisfaction, especially for weaker writers. Newer working papers suggest that once **generative** AI makes tailored text free, written applications lose their signalling value: employers discount them and matching gets less meritocratic. GenAI also homogenises writing. LLM and embedding-based screeners show name-based race/gender bias in audits. Together this argues for AI that polishes and structures **true, evidence-backed content**, rather than AI that invents or inflates.

### Cited Findings
**Writing assistance and hiring (HIGH/MEDIUM)**
- van Inwegen, E., Munyikwa, Z., & Horton, J. J. "Algorithmic Writing Assistance on Jobseekers' Resumes Increases Hires." NBER Working Paper 30886 (2023); arXiv 2301.08083. A secondary source lists it as published in *Management Science* (2025), which I did not confirm on the journal site. The randomized field experiment covered **480,948 jobseekers** on an online labour platform. Treated jobseekers were **hired about 8% more often**, their resumes had fewer spelling and grammar errors, and the gains were concentrated among weaker writers. There was no evidence that employers were less satisfied with the hired workers. The tool was **non-generative** (Grammarly-style), not a ChatGPT-type generator. — [arXiv 2301.08083](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08083v1.pdf); [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2301.08083.html); [MIT Sloan summary](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/node/45245)
- Galdin, A., & Silbert, J. (2025). "Making Talk Cheap: Generative AI and Labor Market Signaling." arXiv 2511.08785 (PREPRINT / job-market paper). Using Freelancer.com data, it finds that after generative AI made customised proposals nearly costless, employers lowered their willingness to pay for customisation. Counterfactuals reportedly show **about 19% fewer high-ability hires** and lower meritocracy, with bottom-quintile workers hired about 14% more often. These figures come from secondary summaries and should be verified in the PDF. — [arXiv 2511.08785](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08785); [Tuck/Dartmouth news](https://tuck.dartmouth.edu/news/articles/what-happens-to-hiring-when-ai-writes-job-applications)
- Cui, J., Dias, ?, & Ye, ? "Signaling in the Age of AI: Evidence from Cover Letters." arXiv 2509.25054 (PREPRINT; I have not confirmed the coauthors' full names). On the same platform, the correlation between cover-letter tailoring and callbacks reportedly **fell by 51%** after AI tools arrived, and AI mattered most for weaker writers. — [author PDF](https://jingyi-cui.github.io/Signaling_AI_Cui_Dias_Ye.pdf); [arXiv 2509.25054](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25054v1)

**Homogenisation (HIGH)**
- Doshi, A. R., & Hauser, O. P. (2024). "Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content." *Science Advances*, 10(28), eadn5290. DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adn5290. In the experiment, 293 writers produced short stories, with or without GenAI ideas, which 600 evaluators rated. GenAI made stories rated more creative and better written, especially for less creative writers, but **AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other**, a social dilemma. The domain is fiction, so transfer to resumes is an inference. — [PMC11244532](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532); [Exeter news](https://news.exeter.ac.uk/?p=13749)

**LLM and embedding screening bias (HIGH/MEDIUM)**
- Wilson, K., & Caliskan, A. (2024). "Gender, Race, and Intersectional Bias in Resume Screening via Language Model Retrieval." *Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society*, 7, 1578–1590. DOI 10.1609/aies.v7i1.31748. The study treats screening as retrieval with three Massive Text Embedding models across 9 occupations, using more than 500 resumes and 500 job descriptions, with race- and gender-signalling names prepended. **Black male names were disadvantaged in up to 100% of cases.** In the authors' Brookings summary, men's and women's names were selected at equal rates in only 37% of tests. Resume length and name frequency affect the measured bias. Removing explicit demographic references is not sufficient. — [AIES/OJS](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31748); [arXiv 2407.20371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20371v2); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/gender-race-and-intersectional-bias-in-ai-resume-screening-via-language-model-retrieval)
- Armstrong, L., Liu, A., MacNeil, S., & Metaxa, D. (2024). "The Silicon Ceiling: Auditing GPT's Race and Gender Biases in Hiring." arXiv 2405.04412 (v3, Nov 2024; PREPRINT on arXiv, which notes a conference version; venue not verified). Its GPT-3.5 audit found stereotype-consistent biases when scoring resumes that differed only by name. When asked to *generate* resumes, GPT gave women's resumes less experienced occupations and gave Asian and Hispanic resumes immigrant markers. — [arXiv 2405.04412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04412v3)
- Bloomberg (Yin, L., Alba, D., & Nicoletti, L., 2024, March). "OpenAI's GPT Is a Recruiter's Dream Tool. Tests Show There's Racial Bias." This is a journalism analysis with code published on GitHub. Equally qualified resumes with demographically distinct names were ranked differently. Names distinct to Black Americans were least likely to be ranked top for financial analyst and **software engineer** roles, and GPT regularly failed adverse-impact benchmarks for several groups. OpenAI responded that out-of-the-box results may not reflect customer use and that businesses can strip names. (Author names are from memory and unverified; the findings come via the article page and search summaries.) — [Bloomberg graphics](https://prod.cm.bloomberg.com/graphics/2024-openai-gpt-hiring-racial-discrimination/); [GitHub code](https://github.com/BloombergGraphics/2024-openai-gpt-hiring-racial-discrimination)
- One critique claims Bloomberg ran no significance tests and that its data show no racial bias. **It comes from interviewing.io, a commercial interview-prep company that sells to job seekers, so under the source policy it is excluded and treated as unverified.** — [interviewing.io](https://interviewing.io/blog/refuting-bloombergs-analysis-chatgpt-isnt-racist)

### Inferences
- Benefit is clearest for **mechanical quality** (errors, clarity), and largest for weaker writers. Many Indian engineers write in English as a second language, so this benefit is plausibly relevant. The platform in van Inwegen et al. was international, but I found no India-specific breakdown.
- Generative tailoring is likely to become **table stakes with falling signal value** (Galdin & Silbert; Cui et al.). The differentiator then shifts to **verifiable specifics**: real metrics, named systems, evidence links, and depth that holds up in a structured interview with follow-up probes (Q2). This supports a system that grounds every generated bullet in a stored record.
- Homogenisation suggests AI-polished bullets will converge on similar phrasing ("spearheaded", "leveraged"). Preserving the user's concrete details and voice is a plausible counter. This is an inference, since no resume-specific homogenisation study was found.
- On fabrication: I found no rigorous study measuring how often LLM resume tools invent achievements. Still, inflated claims are directly exposed by behavioural interviews that probe "what *you* did" and ask for numbers (Amazon, Google). Evans' "exactly as good as it is" principle is consistent with this. Best practice (synthesis): generate only from stored records; attach a source or evidence pointer to each claim; flag any number not present in the record; have the user confirm every metric; and never let the model add tools, scale or outcomes the record lacks.
- On bias exposure, candidates cannot control screener bias. Audits show name signals alone shift rankings and that resume length interacts with bias (Wilson & Caliskan). These are US-centric audits on US race categories. The findings for Indian names or caste/region signals are **unknown**, so transfer to Indian hiring is uncertain.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed RCT of *generative* (LLM) resume writing on hiring outcomes. The strongest RCT (van Inwegen et al.) is non-generative.
- I found no rigorous study of employer perception of resumes they detect or suspect as LLM-written, or of AI-text detection accuracy on resumes, within this session's budget.
- I found no empirical rate of hallucinated or fabricated achievements in AI resume tools. Claims on this appear mainly on vendor or resume-builder sites, which are excluded and treated as unverified.
- I found no audit of LLM screening bias with Indian names, caste, region, college tier or services-company background. This is the key gap for the target user.
- The *Management Science* publication of van Inwegen et al. and the conference venue of Armstrong et al. remain unconfirmed.
