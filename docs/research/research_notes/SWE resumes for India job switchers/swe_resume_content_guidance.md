# What content makes a software engineer's resume effective: employer and university career-centre guidance

Research date: 2026-11-07. Target reader: an engineer with 2-3 years at an Indian IT services company who is applying for SDE-1 or SDE-2 roles at product companies, startups or MNC GCCs in India.

- **EMPLOYER**: published by a hiring employer, on its own domain or as a recruiter quoted in mainstream press.
- **CAREER-CENTRE**: a non-commercial university career office, or a university student society.
- **MIXED**: non-commercial content on a site that also carries affiliate promotions.
- **LOW**: vendor or blog content, or a claim I could not verify.

## Q1. What do hiring employers publish as resume advice for engineers?

### Takeaway
I found substantive, attributable resume advice from only two employers: Google (the X-Y-Z formula) and Amazon (accomplishments over duties, quantify, use the job description's qualifications as keywords). Both put measured accomplishments ahead of responsibilities and want plain, readable formatting. I found no primary guidance from Microsoft, Meta, Atlassian, Stripe, Shopify, Flipkart, Razorpay, Zerodha, Swiggy or GCC employers within my search budget.

### Cited Findings
- **Google, X-Y-Z formula (EMPLOYER via press).** The template is "Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z." A Google software engineer/recruiter presented it in a Google-sponsored YouTube video, and it is meant to keep the experience section concise and force candidates to include metrics and concrete results — [B1 CNBC 2018](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html)
- **Google worked examples** — [B1](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html):
  - "Won second place in Hackathon." becomes "Won second place out of 50 teams in Hackathon with standout productivity tool." The number of entrants supplies the comparison.
  - "Increased server query response time." becomes "Increased server query response time by 15 percent by restructuring our API." This is an engineering example of the formula. As quoted it says "increased" response time, which reads as a wording slip in the source.
  - "Member of Leadership for Tomorrow Society" is rewritten to name the selectivity (one of 275 participants nationwide) and the selection criteria.
- **Other Google points:** keep job descriptions concise. Google reportedly rejects resumes with typos, and vague descriptions are the bigger problem that spellcheck will not catch. Show how you led projects, raised sales or saved money — [B1](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html)
- **Origin of the formula:** it is widely attributed to Laszlo Bock, Google's former SVP of People Operations, in a 2014 LinkedIn post arguing that most resumes lack a concrete measure of past work. I confirmed this only through search-result titles and snippets (Business Insider India). I did not fetch the original LinkedIn post — [B2](https://www.businessinsider.in/google-hr-boss-says-this-is-the-key-to-a-perfect-resume/articleshow/43924179.cms)
- **Google careers site:** the "How we hire" page I fetched (https://www.google.com/about/careers/applications/how-we-hire/) had no resume advice in the retrievable content. Any claim that the formula appears verbatim on Google's careers site is therefore unverified in this research.
- **Amazon India (EMPLOYER).** These quotes are from B3, [aboutamazon.in](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/workplace/amazon-india-job-resume-tips):
  - Anna Duong (Americas Stores Tech Talent Acquisition): "While there are no hard rules, it's best to keep a resume text-forward, black and white, and simple." Recruiters want resumes that are well organised, plainly worded, show measurable data and are easy to scan. They care about accomplishments more than fonts or borders.
  - Bhavishya Lingam (Senior Recruiting Business Partner): "A successful resume should focus on your actions and results."
  - Worked example: "Responsible for introducing new tech stack into our organization" becomes "Successfully led the acquisition and integration of innovative efficiency software that reduced errors by 25%", followed by a 37% year-over-year fall in customer complaints.
  - Not every bullet needs a number, but because Amazon is data-driven, more quantification is better.
  - Patti Cudney: "You don't need a completely different resume for each role you apply to." Use keywords and phrases from the job's "Basic and Preferred Qualifications" to decide what to emphasise.
  - Jen Paradise: "Be sure to include specific stand-out accomplishments of what you've been able to achieve in each role." Responsibilities describe what you did; accomplishments show outcomes.
- **Amazon Leadership Principles.** The Amazon article does not tell candidates to write the Leadership Principles into resume bullets, and it mentions STAR only for interviews (a linked video). The advice to weave Leadership Principles into bullets appears only in third-party guides — [B3](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/workplace/amazon-india-job-resume-tips). Treat that advice as unverified.
- **Amazon recruiter video.** DJ Cabeen's video covers page length, photos, summaries, gaps and tailoring, but the article page carries no text answers — [B4](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/resume-writing-tips-from-an-amazon-recruiter)
- **Indian product companies.** A search for Razorpay, Flipkart, Swiggy and Zerodha resume guidance returned only commercial third-party guides (atsverification, applypilot, resumebuildz, reachrole and others). They make generic, unverified claims such as "scale experience is the #1 differentiator" for Flipkart and "Kafka-heavy" for Swiggy. None of these are company statements. Excluded under the source policy, recorded as LOW and unverified.

### Inferences
- Google and Amazon agree on the core rule: write outcome-first bullets with a measure, and keep formatting plain. Neither asks for a separate resume for every role. Amazon explicitly says you do not need one, but wants emphasis matched to the posting's qualifications.
- Google's own engineering example ("by 15 percent by restructuring our API") shows that a technical metric such as latency counts as a valid "Y". A business or revenue metric is not required.

### Gaps
- No primary resume guidance found from Microsoft, Meta, Atlassian, Stripe, Shopify, Flipkart, Razorpay, Zerodha, Swiggy or GCC employers. Each company's careers FAQ and engineering blog would need a direct search, which was beyond the call budget.
- Google's current careers-site wording could not be retrieved. The fetch returned only navigation.
- The original Laszlo Bock LinkedIn post (2014) was not fetched directly.

## Q2. What do university career centres teach about bullets, structure and sections?

### Takeaway
MIT, Harvard, Columbia, UNC, Illinois and the Illinois HKN society agree on the basics: start with an action verb, write accomplishments rather than duties, quantify where possible, name the technologies used, leave out first-person pronouns, order entries reverse-chronologically, keep to one page early in a career, and tailor using the posting's keywords. Explicit tense rules and per-bullet length limits are rare.

### Cited Findings
- **MIT CAPD** — [B5](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/resumes/):
  - Start from a blank page rather than a template.
  - One page unless you have extensive experience or an advanced degree. Font no smaller than 10pt, margins at least half an inch.
  - Include a Skills section, and also show skills inside experience descriptions so they appear in context.
  - Bullets need not be full sentences. Avoid "I/me/my". "Start descriptions with a strong action verb like built, managed, developed, wrote."
  - Name methods, not just tasks. For software, that means naming the programming language.
  - Describe accomplishments, not duties. Worked contrast: "was responsible for delivering projects on time" becomes "ensured projects were delivered on or ahead of schedule".
  - Quantify, for example audience sizes or money managed.
  - Keep a broader "master resume" and select from it for each application. Use industry and position keywords, because employers may scan for them.
  - Leave out age, religion, health, marital status, photo, salary and references.
- **Harvard Mignone Center** — [B6](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/):
  - Use action verbs and quantifiable results. Be specific, not general; active, not passive.
  - "Write to express, not impress." Be fact-based: "quantify and qualify."
  - No personal pronouns, abbreviations, narrative style or slang.
  - Headings go in order of importance. Entries within a heading are reverse-chronological.
  - Its technical action verbs are Built, Designed, Engineered, Programmed, Optimized and Streamlined.
  - The top five mistakes it lists include passive language and "Not demonstrating results".
  - Use AI tools to work in job-description keywords, but do not let AI be the primary author, because its output "will likely be very generic."
  - The page sets no page limit.
- **Harvard's 2026 CS resume example** uses a "Technical Skills & Projects" section. The engineering example uses a combined "Projects and Skills" heading — [B7](https://cdn-careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/161/2026/07/Harvard-College-CS-Resume-Example.pdf). Seen via search snippets only.
- **Columbia CCE:** start bullets with strong action verbs, add context about scope, and quantify where possible. It notes that not every bullet must be results-oriented — [B10](https://www.careereducation.columbia.edu/resources/resumes-impact-creating-strong-bullet-points). Search summary only; the page returned 403.
- **UNC CS:** quantify with real data and numbers, because that builds credibility with recruiters — [B11](https://cs.unc.edu/experience/career/technical-resumes). Search summary only.
- **Illinois Engineering Career Services:** work job-posting keywords into bullets, for example by naming the specific language you used in a past role — [B12](https://ecs.grainger.illinois.edu/student-resources/resumes-letters/resumes). Search summary only.
- **Illinois HKN, "A-T-O-M" bullet formula (2025)** — [B8](https://wiki.hkn.illinois.edu/more%20advice/1.finding%20an%20internship/):
  - The formula is **A**ction verb, **T**ool/tech, **O**utcome metric, **M**ethod/context.
  - Keep each bullet to two lines or fewer, and aim for about one number every two lines.
  - Worked example: "Refactored Python ETL pipeline, improved throughput 4 × by caching DynamoDB responses."
  - Skills are grouped by category, with at most two soft skills and no skill bar charts.
  - One page, ATS-clean: standard headers, no text boxes or columns, PDF unless the portal asks for DOCX.
- **Tech Interview Handbook (MIXED)** — [B9](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/resume/):
  - Bullet formula: "[Accomplishment summary] : [Action] that resulted in [quantifiable outcome]".
  - Sections in order: headline/summary, contact, skills, experience, education, projects, awards/certifications. Education goes first only for students or people with under 3 years of experience.
  - Headline under 10 words and summary under 50 words, starting with the role noun.
  - Skills format: "[Skill summary] : [skills separated by |]".
  - Experience header format: "Company, Location | Title | MM/YYYY - MM/YYYY".
  - GPA only if above 3.5/4.0. One page.
- **Duties versus accomplishments:** Wilson College's guide says duties tell the hiring manager the scope of the job, while accomplishments show how well it was done — [B15](https://www.wilson.edu/sites/default/files/uploaded/Entry-Level-Resume-Guide.pdf). Snippet only.

**Where sources agree and disagree**
- **Length:** MIT, HKN and the Tech Interview Handbook say one page early in a career. Harvard sets no limit. Amazon says only "concise", with no page count [B3].
- **Summary or headline:** the Tech Interview Handbook recommends one [B9]. MIT, Harvard and HKN do not ask for one, and none of them mentions an objective statement.
- **Section order for someone with 2-3 years of experience:** the Tech Interview Handbook puts experience above education once you are past about 3 years [B9]. Harvard says to order by importance [B6]. These two are compatible.
- **Quantify every bullet?** No. Amazon [B3] and Columbia [B10] both say not every bullet needs a number. HKN suggests "about one number every two lines" [B8].
- **Tense:** none of the fetched sources gives an explicit tense rule. MIT and Harvard are silent.

### Inferences
- For a 2-3 year engineer, the career-centre consensus maps onto a layout of contact, an optional short headline, skills, experience, projects and education. Experience is ordered reverse-chronologically and comes before education.
- A-T-O-M (HKN), X-Y-Z (Google) and the Tech Interview Handbook's formula are variants of the same structure: result plus measure plus method. The difference is whether the result comes first (X-Y-Z) or the action comes first (A-T-O-M). Sources do not say one beats the other.
- The convention of present tense for a current role and past tense for earlier ones is common, but this research did not source it. Treat it as a gap.

### Gaps
- Stanford, CMU, UC Berkeley and Princeton pages were not fetched within the budget. Columbia and UNC were blocked (403).
- IIT Bombay, Madras and Delhi placement-cell resume guidance was not found. It is often hosted behind student logins or in PDFs.
- No explicit tense rule was found in the fetched sources.

## Q3. How should impact be quantified when direct business metrics are missing, and what about "hollow" metrics?

### Takeaway
Technical proxies are explicitly endorsed. Google's own example uses query response time, and HKN lists latency, CPU and memory, throughput, accuracy and test coverage. Relative figures such as "2× faster" are acceptable when absolute numbers are confidential. I found no employer or career-centre source that explicitly addresses "hollow" counts such as tickets or user stories. HKN actually lists "tickets closed" as a business metric, so the case against hollow metrics is not supported by the sources I found.

### Cited Findings
- Google's engineering example quantifies a percentage change in API/server query response time — [B1](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html)
- **HKN metric cheat sheet:**
  - Performance: latency (ms), clock speed, FLOPS.
  - Resource: memory (MB), CPU %.
  - Quality: accuracy %, test coverage.
  - Business: dollars saved or earned, users impacted, tickets closed.
  - "If results are confidential, use relative figures such as '2 × faster.'"
  - The worked example uses throughput (4×).
  
  Source: [B8](https://wiki.hkn.illinois.edu/more%20advice/1.finding%20an%20internship/)
- Amazon's worked examples use error-rate reduction (25%) and complaint reduction (37% year over year) as impact measures — [B3](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/workplace/amazon-india-job-resume-tips)
- Google's hackathon and selection examples show that comparison counts ("out of 50 teams", "one of 275") are a valid measure when no performance metric exists — [B1](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html)
- For project bullets, the Tech Interview Handbook recommends usage numbers (adoption) as scope and impact — [B9](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/resume/)
- MIT suggests quantifying with audience sizes and money managed, and recording process improvements — [B5](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/resumes/)
- UNC says to quantify with real data — [B11](https://cs.unc.edu/experience/career/technical-resumes). Harvard says "be fact-based: quantify and qualify" — [B6](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/)

### Inferences
- Acceptable proxy metrics for a services-company engineer, drawn from the sources above:
  - latency or response time
  - throughput
  - CPU or memory
  - error rate or defect reduction
  - test coverage
  - users impacted or adoption
  - cost saved
  - comparison or selection ratios
  - relative multipliers when absolutes are confidential

  Time saved and reliability (uptime, incidents) follow the same logic but were not named in the fetched sources.
- A raw activity count such as "closed 120 tickets" measures effort rather than an outcome under the X-Y-Z logic (accomplishment, measure, method) and MIT's accomplishments-not-duties rule. This is my inference: no source I found condemns ticket counts explicitly, and HKN lists "tickets closed".
- Harvard's "quantify and qualify" and UNC's "real data" imply that numbers must be defensible. Neither gives a method for honest estimation, such as rounding down, stating a baseline, or using "~".

### Gaps
- No employer or career-centre source found that explicitly discusses "hollow" metrics (ticket, story or defect counts). That discussion may exist only in practitioner communities or commercial resume sites, so it is unverified here.
- No sourced method for estimating a metric honestly when instrumentation was not available.

## Q4. What role do projects, open source, GitHub, competitive programming and certifications play for a 2-3 year engineer moving to product companies?

### Takeaway
Career centres and the Tech Interview Handbook treat projects with GitHub links as standard content. Competitive-programming profiles are optional, and certifications should appear only if they are job-relevant. I found no employer guidance specific to services-to-product switchers.

### Cited Findings
- **Tech Interview Handbook** — [B9](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/resume/):
  - At least two projects, each name linked to its source (for example GitHub), describing scope and impact with usage numbers where available.
  - GitHub, a personal website and "competitive coding profiles with notable achievements" are optional contact items.
  - Awards and certifications only if job-relevant, formatted "Year | quantified result | competition or award name".
  - Worked example: a maintained open-source project with 7.6k+ dependent GitHub projects and named organisations that rely on it.
- **HKN** — [B8](https://wiki.hkn.illinois.edu/more%20advice/1.finding%20an%20internship/):
  - Projects should list the tech stack with 2-3 bullets and a GitHub link after the title.
  - Write "built", not "helped".
  - Pin your strongest repositories, add README screenshots, and optionally a GitHub Pages write-up.
- MIT counts class projects, competitions and personal projects as legitimate experience, provided their relevance is clear — [B5](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/resumes/)
- Harvard's CS example places projects alongside technical skills, under "Technical Skills & Projects" — [B7](https://cdn-careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/161/2026/07/Harvard-College-CS-Resume-Example.pdf)
- Google's hackathon example shows how to frame competition results: give placing and field size — [B1](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/08/16/google-recruiters-share-the-3-part-formula-for-stronger-resumes.html)
- Amazon recruiters advise candidates from other industries to stress transferable skills, noting Amazon "might scale differently than where you've worked in the past." This was seen in a search snippet attributed to aboutamazon.com — [Amazon, "Amazon job application resume writing tips"](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/amazon-job-application-resume-writing-tips). Snippet only, not fetched.

### Inferences
- For a services-company engineer whose client work is under NDA or low on visible metrics, one or two linked, scoped projects give verifiable evidence of skill. The sources treat this as standard, not as a substitute for work experience.
- Competitive-programming ratings and certifications are positioned as optional extras, included only when notable or relevant. No source says they replace projects or work impact.

### Gaps
- No employer source (Indian or global) found on how product companies weigh GitHub, DSA or LeetCode profiles, or certifications, against work experience for lateral hires.
- Claims in Indian commercial guides (for example "portfolios matter more than degrees" from meritshot) are excluded and unverified.

## Q5. How should a resume be tailored to a job description?

### Takeaway
Employers and career centres agree: tailor what you emphasise and the keywords you use to each posting, typically from a master resume. Amazon explicitly says a completely different resume per role is unnecessary. Keyword stuffing is discouraged, and spelling out acronyms is recommended (Tech Interview Handbook).

### Cited Findings
- Amazon: use keywords from "Basic and Preferred Qualifications" to decide emphasis. "You don't need a completely different resume for each role you apply to." — [B3](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/workplace/amazon-india-job-resume-tips)
- MIT: tailor to each position by selecting experiences that demonstrate the skills required, drawing on a master resume. Use industry keywords because employers may scan for them — [B5](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/resumes/)
- Harvard: reference skills and experiences from the job description. AI tools can help incorporate keywords, but should not be the primary author — [B6](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/)
- Illinois ECS: work posting keywords into bullets, for example the specific language used — [B12](https://ecs.grainger.illinois.edu/student-resources/resumes-letters/resumes)
- **Tech Interview Handbook** — [B9](https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/resume/):
  - Mirror job-description language in the skills, experience and education sections.
  - Spell out abbreviations, for example "Amazon Web Services (AWS)".
  - Avoid keyword stuffing.
  - Some ATS infer length of experience from where a skill appears.
  - To generalise for a role type, analyse 3-5 postings for repeated terms.
  
  The ATS claims are unverified against ATS vendor documentation.

### Inferences
- A practical rule set: keep a master resume, then for each application reorder bullets and adjust the skills line so that the posting's required technologies appear where you genuinely used them, inside experience bullets and not only in the skills list. MIT and Illinois both endorse showing skills in context.

### Gaps
- No primary ATS vendor documentation reviewed. Claims about ATS parsing and inferring experience from skill placement remain unverified.

## Q6. Honesty and accuracy: what does guidance say against exaggeration, and what are the consequences, including Indian background verification?

### Takeaway
Career centres stress factual, specific statements ("write to express, not impress"; "quantify with real data"). In Indian IT, background verification (BGV) is standard practice and the NASSCOM National Skills Registry exists to support it. Specific termination cases and fraud-prevalence figures circulate mainly on vendor and legal sites and are not verified against primary reporting.

### Cited Findings
- Harvard: "Write to express, not impress", "be fact-based: quantify and qualify", and keep language articulate, not "flowery" — [B6](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/create-a-strong-resume/)
- UNC: quantify with real data and numbers, since that builds credibility — [B11](https://cs.unc.edu/experience/career/technical-resumes)
- The National Skills Registry site displays NASSCOM, NSR and NSDL/NDML branding. It reports 25,86,677 IT Professional Identification Numbers generated, 337 subscriber companies and 25 background checkers, and it refers to "EBC Verification". It does not describe on the home page what records are held — [B13](https://nationalskillsregistry.com/)
- Search-result summaries (not fetched) say NASSCOM set up the NSR in 2005 and NSDL maintains it. They also say BGV is not legally mandatory for most private employers in India but is industry-standard in IT/ITES — [B13](https://nationalskillsregistry.com/); [B14 NovoJuris](https://www.novojuris.com/thought-leadership/background-verification-of-employees-does-it-cover-the-secrets.html). LOW confidence.
- A law-firm article (NovoJuris) reportedly states that Infosys terminated about a hundred employees and TCS twenty over resume discrepancies. I saw this only as a search snippet and it is not verified against news reports — [B14](https://www.novojuris.com/thought-leadership/background-verification-of-employees-does-it-cover-the-secrets.html). LOW confidence.
- Prevalence figures such as "up to 10% of resumes falsified, per NASSCOM" appear only in vendor marketing, with no primary NASSCOM source found. Unverified; do not use.

### Inferences
- For the target user, overstated titles, dates or employers carry a concrete risk because BGV is routine in Indian IT hiring. Inflated metrics are a softer risk: they are not checked by BGV but can be probed in interviews. The interview-probing point is my inference and is not sourced here.
- A defensible-numbers rule (use only figures you can explain, with a baseline) follows from Harvard's and UNC's emphasis on factual, real data. The specific method is not sourced.
